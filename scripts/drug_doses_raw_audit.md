## 1. Propofol (Diprivan) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Alkylphenol (2,6-diisopropylphenol) • GABA-A receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: The default IV induction agent almost everywhere — fast on, fast off, and pleasant to wake up from
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Diprivan (propofol) injectable emulsion label (Fresenius Kabi/AstraZeneca); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for induction in a healthy adult under 55, 2–2.5 mg/kg IV, given in small increments (about 40 mg every 10 seconds) rather than as one push — go slower and use less in the elderly, the debilitated, or anyone ASA III/IV, since they tolerate the blood pressure drop far less well. For maintenance, 100–200 mcg/kg/min as an infusion, titrated to effect. For monitored sedation outside the OR, start lower still — 25–75 mcg/kg/min, or a slow 0.5 mg/kg bolus over 3–5 minutes. In a ventilated ICU patient, start at 5 mcg/kg/min and titrate up in small steps rather than loading quickly.
- **Stated Off-Label**: A sub-hypnotic dose (10–20 mg IV) is a well-known rescue treatment for PONV when standard antiemetics haven't worked. It's also used, again off-label, for procedural sedation by trained non-anaesthesia staff under many institutional protocols, and as a continuous infusion for refractory status epilepticus when first-line agents have failed.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The two things that will bite you fastest are hypotension and apnoea — both dose-dependent, both expected, both why you never give propofol without full monitoring and a plan for the airway. Injection pain is common and can be blunted by giving it in a large vein or mixing in a little lidocaine first. The one complication worth knowing cold, even though it's rare, is Propofol Infusion Syndrome — metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, hyperkalaemia and cardiac failure from prolonged high-dose infusion, seen more in children, in critically ill patients, and with very high infusion rates sustained for days rather than hours. And because it's a lipid emulsion, strict aseptic handling matters more here than with almost any other anaesthetic drug — an opened vial left standing is a genuine bacterial growth risk, not just a theoretical one.

## 2. Etomidate (Amidate) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Carboxylated imidazole ester • Positive allosteric GABA-A modulator
- **Tagline**: The induction agent you reach for when the heart can't afford much of a hit
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Amidate (etomidate) injection label (Hospira/Pfizer); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: 0.2–0.6 mg/kg IV as a single induction dose, given over 30–60 seconds — most adults land around 0.3 mg/kg. The label doesn't cover it for maintenance infusion or repeat dosing, and that's a real restriction, not just a formality, given the adrenal suppression risk with repeated exposure.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's sometimes chosen, carefully and with the debate around it acknowledged, as a single induction dose for rapid sequence induction in septic or otherwise critically ill patients specifically because of its haemodynamic stability — the argument being that even a single-dose adrenal effect is a smaller risk than a hypotensive crash at induction in someone with almost no cardiovascular reserve. It also sees use as an induction agent in high-risk cardiac patients undergoing procedural sedation where blood pressure swings are poorly tolerated.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Adrenal suppression is the headline issue — real, measurable, and the reason many units now give empiric stress-dose steroids after using etomidate in critically ill or septic patients, even though the single-dose clinical significance is still debated in the literature. Myoclonus is common and can look alarming — it's disinhibition of subcortical structures, not a seizure, and it's blunted by pre-treating with an opioid or benzodiazepine. Pain on injection and superficial phlebitis are common. PONV is more frequent than with propofol. On the plus side, it causes little histamine release and comparatively little respiratory depression, though apnoea can still happen, especially with concurrent opioids.

## 3. Ketamine (Ketalar) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Arylcycloalkylamine (phencyclidine congener) • Non-competitive NMDA receptor antagonist
- **Tagline**: The odd one out — it provides its own analgesia, keeps the patient breathing, and raises the blood pressure instead of dropping it
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Ketalar (ketamine hydrochloride) injection label (Par Pharmaceutical); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for induction, 1–4.5 mg/kg IV given over about a minute, or 6.5–13 mg/kg IM if IV access isn't available. Maintenance is achieved with repeat doses of half to a full induction dose, or a continuous infusion, titrated to effect.
- **Stated Off-Label**: At much lower, sub-dissociative doses — typically 0.1–0.3 mg/kg IV, or a low-dose infusion — it's a genuinely effective analgesic for acute and chronic refractory pain, and a popular opioid-sparing addition to a multimodal perioperative pain plan. It's also used for procedural sedation in emergency departments, as an induction agent of choice in status asthmaticus refractory to standard bronchodilators, and — as esketamine specifically — is FDA-approved in its own right for treatment-resistant depression, a genuinely separate indication from its anaesthetic use. Intranasal (roughly 3–6 mg/kg) or oral (roughly 5–10 mg/kg, often mixed into a flavoured drink) racemic ketamine is a well-established off-label option for paediatric premedication or procedural sedation when IV access isn't yet in place and IM administration isn't wanted.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Emergence phenomena — vivid dreams, hallucinations, a dysphoric 'coming back' experience — are the most talked-about downside, and they're meaningfully reduced by co-administering a benzodiazepine and by keeping the recovery environment quiet and undisturbed. It raises heart rate, blood pressure and myocardial oxygen demand, which makes it a poor choice in a patient with poorly controlled ischaemic heart disease or severe hypertension. It increases salivation enough that an antisialagogue is often given beforehand, and despite 'preserved' airway reflexes, laryngospasm from those secretions can still occur. It raises intraocular pressure. And because it has real recreational misuse potential, it's a Schedule III controlled substance in the US and needs to be tracked and stored accordingly.

## 4. Thiopental (Sodium Thiopental) (Pentothal) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Thiobarbiturate (sulfur-substituted barbituric acid) • GABA-A channel potentiator
- **Tagline**: The original rapid-acting induction agent — historically important, no longer available in the US
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information for Pentothal (thiopental sodium), last US-marketed formulation (Hospira) — product discontinued from the US market in 2011; UpToDate "Barbiturates" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed., Ch. 21.
- **Stated Dosage**: Historic FDA-approved dosing: 3–5 mg/kg IV for induction in a healthy adult, titrated, with lower doses in the elderly or hypovolaemic. It is no longer available in the US market — the sole remaining manufacturer, Hospira, discontinued it in 2011, for reasons tied to manufacturing and its use in lethal injection — though it's retained here because it still appears in some other countries' practice and remains a fixture of exam and historical teaching.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Where it's still available, it's occasionally used for status epilepticus refractory to first- and second-line agents, and for therapeutic burst suppression in refractory raised intracranial pressure — roles largely taken over by propofol elsewhere.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Extravasation or accidental intra-arterial injection causes severe tissue necrosis and thrombosis — historically managed by injecting local anaesthetic or a vasodilator into the same artery to limit the damage. It causes more histamine release than propofol, so laryngospasm and bronchospasm are more of a concern. It's absolutely contraindicated in acute intermittent porphyria — barbiturates induce ALA synthase and can trigger a life-threatening attack. And because of its long elimination half-life, repeated dosing causes genuinely prolonged recovery in a way that simply doesn't happen with propofol.

## 5. Midazolam (Versed) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Water-soluble imidazobenzodiazepine • GABA-A positive allosteric modulator
- **Tagline**: The benzodiazepine anaesthetists actually use — for calming nerves before a case, not usually for the induction itself
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Versed (midazolam) injection label (Pfizer); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for premedication, 1–2 mg IV increments (or 0.07–0.08 mg/kg IM), titrated to effect. For procedural sedation, small increments of 0.5–2 mg IV, titrated, usually totalling under 5 mg. For induction of general anaesthesia, 0.1–0.3 mg/kg IV — less if the patient's already had opioid premedication, or if they're elderly or high-risk. For continuous ICU sedation, 0.02–0.1 mg/kg/h as an infusion.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Intranasal or buccal midazolam (roughly 0.2–0.3 mg/kg, maximum around 10 mg) is widely used, particularly in children, for anxiolytic premedication before a case when IV access isn't yet in place. A separate, FDA-approved intranasal product (Nayzilam) exists for a genuinely different indication — rescue treatment of intermittent, stereotypic seizure clusters — dosed as 5 mg (one spray) into one nostril, repeatable once after 10 minutes if needed. Intramuscular midazolam at the same premedication dose is a standard option for acute agitation. And it's a common component of continuous infusion regimens for refractory status epilepticus.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Respiratory depression is the big one, especially combined with opioids — always ask what else the patient has on board before you dose it. Paradoxical agitation happens, more often in children and the elderly, and can look confusingly like undersedation if you don't recognise it for what it is. Recovery can be genuinely prolonged in the elderly, the obese, anyone with hepatic impairment, or with a CYP3A4 inhibitor on board (certain antifungals and macrolides, for instance). Its anterograde amnesia is usually the whole point, but it's worth remembering it's happening if a patient seems oddly unbothered by something distressing. And if things do go too far, flumazenil is the specific reversal agent for benzodiazepine-induced sedation and respiratory depression.

## 6. Remimazolam (Byfavo) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Ester-hydrolysed soft-drug benzodiazepine • Tissue carboxylesterase-cleared GABA-A modulator
- **Tagline**: A benzodiazepine engineered to be broken down almost instantly — sedation you can turn off nearly as fast as you turned it on
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Byfavo (remimazolam besylate) injection label (Acacia Pharma/Cosette Pharmaceuticals), approved July 2, 2020; UpToDate "Remimazolam: Drug information" (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for procedural sedation in adults undergoing a procedure lasting 30 minutes or less, an initial dose of 5 mg IV given over roughly 1 minute, followed by 2.5 mg top-up doses as needed, titrated to the desired level of sedation. It is not currently FDA-approved as a primary general anaesthesia induction agent — that use, seen in research and in some countries' broader clinical practice, sits outside its US label.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Its use as an induction and maintenance agent for general anaesthesia — sometimes combined with an opioid such as sufentanil — is an active area of research and adopted practice in some settings, exploiting the same fast, organ-independent offset that makes it attractive for procedural sedation, though this remains outside its current FDA-approved indication.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: As a benzodiazepine, it carries the same class-wide risks as midazolam — dose-dependent respiratory depression, markedly potentiated by opioids, and hypotension, particularly in combination with other sedating or cardiodepressant drugs. Because its offset is so fast and so reliable, under-sedation or a patient rousing mid-procedure is arguably a more practical concern than prolonged sedation. It's a Schedule IV controlled substance in the US. And as with any newer agent, the safety experience — while reassuring so far — is simply smaller in volume than midazolam's decades of use, which is worth keeping in mind rather than assuming an identical risk profile purely because it's 'a benzodiazepine.'

## 7. Cipepofol (Ciprofol) (Cypsedo) — [Category: INDUCTION]
- **Classification**: Chiral cyclopropyl alkylphenol • High-potency GABA-A receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: The newest FDA-approved general anaesthesia induction agent — a fluorinated propofol relative that works at a fraction of the dose
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Cypsedo (cipepofol) injection label (Haisco Pharmaceutical Group / HaiscoUSA Pharmaceuticals), approved May 29, 2026; From propofol to cipepofol (Cypsedo®): Medicinal Chemistry Research (2026); UpToDate general anaesthesia induction agent updates (2026).
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved (2026): for induction of general anaesthesia in adults undergoing surgery, dosing is weight-based and further adjusted by the patient's age group, BMI category, and ASA physical status classification — reflecting its narrow, potency-adjusted dosing window compared with propofol's simpler flat mg/kg dosing. It is supplied as a 50 mg/20 mL (2.5 mg/mL) single-dose vial. Because this is a newly approved agent, always confirm the exact current weight/age/ASA-adjusted dosing table against the live FDA label before clinical use rather than relying on a fixed number from memory.
- **Stated Off-Label**: As the newest agent in this class, formally studied off-label uses are still emerging in the literature; extrapolating from propofol, it is reasonable to expect future study of sub-hypnotic antiemetic dosing and procedural sedation applications, but neither is established or FDA-approved for cipepofol at this time — don't assume propofol's off-label uses transfer automatically.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Expect a safety profile that closely parallels propofol's — dose-dependent hypotension and respiratory depression requiring the same monitoring and airway readiness, and injection-related discomfort, though early studies suggest injection pain may be somewhat less prominent than with propofol, plausibly related to the lower volume and dose required. As with any agent so newly approved (May 2026), the depth of real-world safety experience is inevitably far smaller than propofol's decades of use — treat it with the same vigilance you'd apply to any new drug, and don't assume every nuance of propofol's safety profile (including its Propofol Infusion Syndrome risk with prolonged high-dose use) has been fully characterised for cipepofol yet.

## 8. Succinylcholine (Suxamethonium) (Anectine) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Bis-quaternary acetylcholine dimer (ultra-short-acting)
- **Tagline**: Still the fastest paralytic in the drawer — and the only depolarising one, which explains both its speed and its risks
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Anectine (succinylcholine chloride) injection label (Hospira/Pfizer); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is approximately 0.3 mg/kg IV in adults. FDA-approved dosing: for rapid sequence induction/intubation (RSI) in adults, 1–1.5 mg/kg IV (representing 3 to 5 times the ED95, guaranteeing complete 100% vocal cord paralysis within 30–60 seconds with a recovery duration of 5–10 minutes). For shorter routine procedures, 0.3–1.1 mg/kg IV. When IV access isn't available — classically, severe paediatric laryngospasm — it can be given IM at up to 3–4 mg/kg (maximum 150 mg, onset 3–4 minutes). A continuous infusion (2.5 mg/min, titrated) is rarely used today given the risk of drifting into a Phase II block.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Its best-known off-label use is treating severe laryngospasm that hasn't responded to positive-pressure ventilation and jaw thrust — often given IM or intraosseously if IV access isn't yet secured. This is a well-established emergency manoeuvre, just not the drug's primary labelled indication.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The FDA carries a black-box warning on this drug for good reason: hyperkalaemic cardiac arrest, particularly in children with undiagnosed skeletal-muscle myopathy — which is why routine paediatric use is now restricted to emergency airway control rather than elective cases. The hyperkalaemia risk extends well beyond children too: burns (from about 24 hours after injury and for up to a couple of years), spinal cord injury, stroke, prolonged immobility, severe intra-abdominal sepsis, and any neuromuscular disease all meaningfully raise the risk. It's also a recognised trigger for malignant hyperthermia. Masseter muscle rigidity after succinylcholine can be an early warning sign of MH and deserves to be taken seriously, not brushed off. Bradycardia and other arrhythmias occur, especially with repeat dosing in children — often pre-empted with atropine. Myalgia afterward is common, likely related to the fasciculations. And in someone with pseudocholinesterase deficiency, paralysis can run dramatically longer than expected, requiring ventilatory support until it wears off.

## 9. Rocuronium (Zemuron) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Monoquaternary aminosteroid (intermediate-acting)
- **Tagline**: The non-depolarising relaxant fast enough to substitute for succinylcholine at RSI — and the one sugammadex was built for
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Zemuron (rocuronium bromide) injection label (Merck); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is 0.3 mg/kg IV in adults under balanced anaesthesia. FDA-approved dosing: for routine tracheal intubation, 0.6 mg/kg IV (representing exactly 2 times the ED95, producing excellent intubating conditions in 60–90 seconds with an intermediate clinical duration of 30–40 minutes). For rapid sequence induction (RSI), 0.9–1.2 mg/kg IV (3 to 4 times the ED95, bringing onset down to roughly 60 seconds to match succinylcholine, with an extended clinical duration of 50–90 minutes). For maintenance boluses, 0.1–0.15 mg/kg IV (roughly 0.3 to 0.5 times the ED95) as needed, or a continuous infusion of 0.01 mg/kg/min (5–12 mcg/kg/min), titrated against a peripheral nerve stimulator. The label also covers IM use in infants and children when IV access isn't available and intubation isn't emergent — roughly 1 mg/kg in infants, 1.8 mg/kg in children — but onset is far slower (three to four minutes or more) and duration meaningfully longer than the IV route, so IM rocuronium is not suitable for rapid sequence induction.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Its high-dose use specifically to enable a reliable RSI when succinylcholine is contraindicated — hyperkalaemia risk, MH susceptibility — is now such standard, well-supported practice that it barely feels 'off-label', but the specific high-dose RSI regimen does technically sit outside the base labelled intubating-dose range.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The most common real-world problem is residual paralysis at extubation if reversal wasn't adequate or wasn't checked — a genuinely major contributor to postoperative pulmonary complications, and entirely avoidable with quantitative train-of-four monitoring before pulling the tube. It's also become one of the neuromuscular blockers most frequently implicated in perioperative anaphylaxis across several national allergy registries, worth remembering the first time hypotension and bronchospasm appear shortly after induction. Hepatic failure meaningfully prolongs its duration. And it's the aminosteroid that sugammadex — the reversal agent covered separately — was essentially designed around, giving it a reversal option no other relaxant on this list has in quite the same reliable, depth-independent way.

## 10. Vecuronium (Norcuron) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Monoquaternary aminosteroid (intermediate-acting)
- **Tagline**: Rocuronium's quieter older sibling — same steroid family, gentler on the heart, a bit slower
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Norcuron (vecuronium bromide) injection label (brand discontinued; generic vecuronium bromide for injection remains FDA-approved) (originally Organon); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is 0.043 to 0.05 mg/kg IV in adults. FDA-approved dosing: for initial tracheal intubation, 0.08–0.1 mg/kg IV (representing approximately 2 times the ED95, achieving good to excellent intubating conditions in 2.5–3 minutes and a clinical duration of 25–40 minutes). For maintenance during prolonged surgery, 0.01–0.015 mg/kg IV increments (approximately 0.2 to 0.3 times the ED95) given when first twitch of train-of-four returns, or a continuous infusion around 0.001 mg/kg/min (1 mcg/kg/min, or 0.8–1.2 mcg/kg/min), titrated against quantitative neuromuscular monitoring.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's used as a continuous infusion for neuromuscular blockade in ICU settings — severe ARDS with ventilator dyssynchrony, refractory status asthmaticus — though cisatracurium is now often preferred there specifically because its clearance doesn't depend on organ function the way vecuronium's does.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Prolonged or residual blockade is the main concern, and it shows up in two distinct settings: hepatic or renal impairment slowing clearance of a single dose, and prolonged ICU infusion, where there's also a recognised association with ICU-acquired weakness, particularly when corticosteroids are on board at the same time. Because it's so haemodynamically quiet, unexplained bradycardia or hypotension during a vecuronium-based case should prompt you to look elsewhere for the cause rather than blaming the relaxant.

## 11. Atracurium (Tracrium) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Bis-quaternary benzylisoquinolinium (intermediate-acting, Hofmann elimination)
- **Tagline**: The relaxant that breaks itself down chemically, independent of liver or kidney function — at the cost of some histamine release
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Tracrium (atracurium besylate) injection label (originally GlaxoSmithKline); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is 0.2 to 0.23 mg/kg IV in adults. FDA-approved dosing: for tracheal intubation, an initial dose of 0.4–0.5 mg/kg IV (representing approximately 2 times the ED95, producing intubating conditions in 2–3 minutes and a clinical duration of 30–45 minutes). For maintenance, 0.08–0.1 mg/kg IV as needed (roughly 0.3 to 0.4 times the ED95), or a continuous infusion of 5–10 mcg/kg/min (0.3–0.6 mg/kg/h), titrated against neuromuscular monitoring. Injections should be administered slowly over 60 seconds to minimise mast-cell histamine release.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Historically, atracurium (alongside cisatracurium) was specifically favoured for patients with combined hepatic and renal failure precisely because of its organ-independent Hofmann clearance — cisatracurium has largely taken over that role today, mainly because it causes far less histamine release at equivalent potency.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Histamine-mediated flushing, hypotension and bronchospasm are more pronounced with atracurium than with cisatracurium or vecuronium, so it's worth injecting it slowly, particularly in anyone with reactive airway disease or borderline haemodynamics. Laudanosine-related CNS excitation is a theoretical concern with very prolonged high-dose infusion but is rarely clinically significant at standard dosing. Benzylisoquinoliniums as a class do turn up in perioperative anaphylaxis case series, though less often than rocuronium in most registries.

## 12. Cisatracurium (Nimbex) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Purified 1R-cis,1'R-cis benzylisoquinolinium (intermediate-acting, Hofmann elimination)
- **Tagline**: Atracurium's cleaner isomer — same organ-independent breakdown, almost none of the histamine release
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Nimbex (cisatracurium besylate) injection label (AbbVie); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is 0.05 mg/kg IV in adults during balanced opioid/nitrous anaesthesia. FDA-approved dosing: for intubation, 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV (representing 3 to 4 times the ED95; 0.15 mg/kg gives onset in roughly 2 minutes with a 45–60 minute duration, while 0.2 mg/kg accelerates onset to 1.5–2 minutes with a 55–65 minute duration). For maintenance, 0.03 mg/kg IV as needed (roughly 0.6 times the ED95, adding ~20 minutes of block), or a continuous maintenance infusion of 1–3 mcg/kg/min (0.06–0.18 mg/kg/h), titrated against train-of-four monitoring — the same approach used for ICU infusions, just with closer, more regular monitoring given how long those infusions typically run.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a first-line choice for continuous neuromuscular blockade in severe ARDS with ventilator dyssynchrony or during proning — supported by trial evidence for short-course early paralysis in severe ARDS — and for refractory status asthmaticus, favoured over the aminosteroids largely because of that organ-independent clearance during a prolonged infusion.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Even with organ-independent clearance, prolonged ICU infusion can still cause prolonged block, particularly with concurrent corticosteroids, which raises the risk of ICU-acquired weakness or critical illness myopathy — the same risk seen with vecuronium infusions, not something cisatracurium is immune to. Laudanosine accumulation is theoretically possible but clinically negligible at standard dosing. Because histamine release is minimal, unexplained hypotension during its use is unlikely to be the drug itself — look elsewhere first. Ongoing quantitative neuromuscular monitoring remains essential during any prolonged infusion.

## 13. Pancuronium (Pavulon) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Bis-quaternary aminosteroid (long-acting, vagolytic)
- **Tagline**: The long-acting relaxant that speeds the heart up instead of leaving it alone — occasionally exactly what you want
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Pavulon (pancuronium bromide) injection label (brand largely discontinued; generic pancuronium bromide remains available) (originally Organon); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is 0.06 to 0.07 mg/kg IV in adults. FDA-approved dosing: for initial tracheal intubation, 0.08–0.1 mg/kg IV (representing approximately 1.5 to 2 times the ED95, producing intubation conditions within 2.5–4 minutes with a long clinical duration of 60–100 minutes). For maintenance during prolonged procedures, 0.01–0.02 mg/kg IV as needed, titrated against peripheral nerve stimulation. It is not recommended for continuous infusion due to significant accumulation and delayed post-surgical recovery.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's occasionally chosen specifically for its vagolytic, heart-rate-raising effect during high-dose opioid cardiac anaesthesia, to offset the bradycardia opioids like fentanyl or sufentanil produce at high doses — a genuine, recognised practice, though shorter-acting relaxants with better reversal options have largely taken over this role.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Prolonged or residual paralysis is a bigger problem with pancuronium than with the intermediate-acting relaxants, particularly in renal failure or with extended ICU use — historically it carried a meaningfully higher rate of postoperative residual paralysis than shorter-acting agents. Tachycardia and hypertension from its vagolytic effect make it a poor choice where tachycardia is poorly tolerated — significant coronary disease, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Reversal with neostigmine is slower and less complete than for the intermediate-acting relaxants. And it's worth knowing that current evidence and labelling for sugammadex is built around and validated for the aminosteroids rocuronium and vecuronium specifically — data supporting its use to reverse pancuronium are considerably more limited, so don't assume the same reliable reversal applies here.

## 14. Mivacurium (Mivacron) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Benzylisoquinolinium diester (short-acting, plasma cholinesterase cleared)
- **Tagline**: The shortest-acting non-depolariser ever marketed — cleared by the same enzyme as succinylcholine, and just as vulnerable to its deficiency
- **Source Citation**: Mivacron (mivacurium chloride) injection label (brand discontinued in the US; included for comparative/exam relevance) — originally GlaxoWellcome; UpToDate and Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed., neuromuscular blockade chapters.
- **Stated Dosage**: ED95 is 0.07 to 0.08 mg/kg IV in adults. Historic FDA-approved dosing: for initial tracheal intubation, 0.15–0.2 mg/kg IV (representing 2 to 2.5 times the ED95, achieving intubating conditions in 2–3 minutes and a short clinical duration of 15–20 minutes, or administered as divided doses of 0.15 mg/kg followed 30 seconds later by 0.1 mg/kg to minimise histamine release). For maintenance, 0.1 mg/kg IV as needed, or a continuous infusion of 4–10 mcg/kg/min, titrated against train-of-four monitoring.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Where it was available, it was a labelled (not off-label) choice for short procedures needing brief non-depolarising relaxation — laryngoscopy, bronchoscopy, short laparoscopic cases — as an alternative to succinylcholine when a non-depolarising block was preferred for the same short duration.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Prolonged block in plasma cholinesterase deficiency, for exactly the same genetic and acquired reasons that prolong succinylcholine's effect (liver disease, pregnancy, certain drugs, homozygous atypical enzyme variants). Histamine-related hypotension, flushing and bronchospasm with rapid injection. And a detail worth remembering precisely because it's easy to get backwards: mivacurium is a benzylisoquinolinium, not an aminosteroid, so sugammadex does not reverse it — recovery depends on spontaneous cholinesterase-driven breakdown, or, once sufficient spontaneous recovery has already begun, an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor like neostigmine.

## 15. Gantacurium (Investigational — no marketed brand) — [Category: RELAXANTS]
- **Classification**: Non-depolarising neuromuscular blocker • Asymmetric chlorofumarate (investigational ultra-short, L-cysteine reversible)
- **Tagline**: An experimental relaxant designed to be reversed in seconds by a simple IV amino acid — never actually reached the market
- **Source Citation**: Investigational agent — not FDA-approved and not currently in active clinical development. Sourced from the peer-reviewed literature, not a product label: Naguib M, et al. "New Drug Developments for Neuromuscular Blockade and Reversal: Gantacurium, CW002, CW011, and Calabadion," Anesthesia & Analgesia (2018); Lien CA, Savard P, et al., mechanism-of-reversal studies (Anesthesiology, 2009–2010); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed., neuromuscular blockade chapter.
- **Stated Dosage**: Investigational ED95 in human trials is approximately 0.19 mg/kg IV. There is no FDA-approved dosing because gantacurium was never commercially approved. Doses studied in Phase 1/2 clinical trials ranged from 2 to 3 times the ED95 (0.35–0.54 mg/kg IV, which achieved tracheal intubation within 60–90 seconds with a spontaneous recovery duration of 5–10 minutes, reversed in under 2 minutes by 10 mg/kg IV L-cysteine adduction). These experimental trial regimens are documented for comparative pharmacology and exam revision only, and must never be used clinically.
- **Stated Off-Label**: None — it has no approved indication to be 'off-label' from, and it is not available for clinical use.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The dose-dependent histamine release seen in human trials — with genuine hypotension and tachycardia at clinically effective doses — is precisely what halted its clinical development, despite an otherwise attractive ultra-fast onset/offset profile and a genuinely novel cysteine-based reversal mechanism. It's worth knowing this story less for gantacurium itself and more for what it teaches: an analogue in the same fumarate family, CW002, was subsequently developed specifically to avoid this histamine problem and has shown a more favourable safety profile in animal and early clinical study, though as of the most recent literature it has not reached FDA approval either. If you're asked about 'newer neuromuscular blockers' expecting an approved answer, sugammadex-reversible rocuronium and vecuronium remain the actual current standard — gantacurium is a well-studied dead end, not a drug you'll ever draw up.

## 16. Sugammadex (Bridion) — [Category: REVERSAL]
- **Classification**: Modified gamma-cyclodextrin • Selective relaxant binding agent (SRBA)
- **Tagline**: It doesn't inhibit an enzyme like neostigmine does — it physically grabs the relaxant molecule and pulls it out of the picture
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Bridion (sugammadex) injection label (Merck); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved, dosed by actual body weight: for reversing a moderate block (defined as reappearance of T2 on train-of-four), 2 mg/kg IV. For a deep block (post-tetanic count of 1–2, no train-of-four response at all), 4 mg/kg IV. For immediate reversal — given 3 minutes after a 1.2 mg/kg RSI dose of rocuronium — 16 mg/kg IV, a genuinely large dose reserved for exactly that scenario.
- **Stated Off-Label**: That 16 mg/kg dose, labelled for immediate reversal shortly after an RSI dose of rocuronium, is also the dose reached for in a true 'can't intubate, can't oxygenate' rescue scenario — using sugammadex to rapidly restore spontaneous ventilation when the airway plan has failed. It's the same labelled dose applied to a slightly different, genuinely emergency, clinical trigger.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Hypersensitivity reactions, including anaphylaxis, are a recognised and sometimes severe risk — the FDA label carries a specific warning about this, and it's worth taking seriously rather than treating sugammadex as risk-free just because its mechanism is novel. Marked bradycardia, including rare reports of cardiac arrest shortly after administration, has also been reported post-marketing, so monitoring after giving it matters. It transiently reduces the effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives, because it also binds progestogens — patients of reproductive age need to be told to use additional non-hormonal contraception for a period afterward. It can transiently prolong aPTT and PT. And there's a genuinely practical interaction worth remembering: after sugammadex has been given, re-establishing paralysis with rocuronium or vecuronium can be difficult or require unusually high doses for some time afterward — if you need to re-paralyse a patient soon after reversal, a non-aminosteroid relaxant is generally the better choice. It also does not reverse the benzylisoquinolinium relaxants — atracurium, cisatracurium, mivacurium.

## 17. Neostigmine (Prostigmin) — [Category: REVERSAL]
- **Classification**: Quaternary ammonium carbamate • Reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor
- **Tagline**: The older reversal strategy — flood the neuromuscular junction with acetylcholine and let it out-compete the relaxant
- **Source Citation**: UpToDate "Prostigmin (neostigmine methylsulfate)" drug information; Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; generic neostigmine widely available.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for reversing non-depolarising block, 0.5–2.5 mg IV, commonly dosed around 0.03–0.07 mg/kg, up to a total of roughly 5 mg, given together with an antimuscarinic (glycopyrrolate, about 0.2 mg per 1 mg of neostigmine, or atropine, 0.4–0.8 mg per 1 mg of neostigmine). There's a genuine ceiling here worth knowing — around 0.07 mg/kg or 5 mg total — beyond which more neostigmine doesn't antagonise the block any further and can actually make neuromuscular function worse instead of better.
- **Stated Off-Label**: A well-established off-label use is acute colonic pseudo-obstruction (Ogilvie syndrome) — given as a slow, cardiac-monitored IV infusion (2 mg over 3–5 minutes) to exploit its parasympathomimetic prokinetic effect on colonic smooth muscle, a completely different clinical scenario from neuromuscular reversal. Oral neostigmine also has its own separate, labelled indication for myasthenia gravis.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The single most important thing to get right is timing relative to the depth of block: giving neostigmine when there's no spontaneous recovery yet ('deep block') simply doesn't work, and can paradoxically worsen the block through excess-acetylcholine desensitisation at the junction — always confirm some spontaneous recovery on a nerve stimulator before giving it. Bradycardia, even asystole, can occur if the antimuscarinic dose or timing doesn't match. Bronchospasm and increased secretions are a real concern in anyone with reactive airway disease. Nausea and increased bowel motility are common, and some surgical contexts raise (debated) concern about anastomotic leak risk from that increased motility. And it's worth remembering it does nothing for a depolarising (succinylcholine) block — in fact, by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase and to some extent plasma cholinesterase too, giving neostigmine while succinylcholine is still working can actually prolong its effect rather than reverse it.

## 18. Fentanyl (Sublimaze) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Synthetic 4-anilidopiperidine (phenylpiperidine family)
- **Tagline**: The workhorse perioperative opioid — fast on, gentle on the heart, but sneaky with repeated dosing
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Sublimaze (fentanyl citrate) injection label (Akorn/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved, and deliberately wide because it spans everything from a minor procedure to a full cardiac case: as an adjunct to general anaesthesia, 2 mcg/kg IV for minor procedures, 2–20 mcg/kg IV for more stimulating surgery, and 20–50 mcg/kg IV as a primary anaesthetic component for major procedures like cardiac surgery. Fentanyl citrate injection may also be given IM at the equivalent IV dose when IV access isn't practical. For titrating postoperative or procedural analgesia, 25–100 mcg IV increments as needed. Separately labelled products exist for other routes and other indications: Duragesic transdermal patches (12–100 mcg/h) for chronic pain, and intranasal/transmucosal products (Lazanda, Actiq and similar) for breakthrough cancer pain specifically in opioid-tolerant patients — none of these chronic-pain formulations are interchangeable with perioperative dosing.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Neuraxial fentanyl — added to local anaesthetic for labour or postoperative epidural and spinal analgesia — is extremely common practice, technically off-label relative to its IV/IM/transdermal/transmucosal labelling, but extensively studied and well established: epidural, typically 50–100 mcg as a bolus or 2–5 mcg/mL added to a local anaesthetic infusion; intrathecal, typically 10–25 mcg as a single-shot adjunct (classic in a combined spinal-epidural). It's also a standard component of IV PCA regimens. Intranasal fentanyl at acute-pain doses (roughly 1–2 mcg/kg) is widely used off-label for breakthrough or procedural analgesia when IV access isn't available, particularly in paediatric emergency departments and prehospital care — distinct from the labelled Lazanda product, which is dosed and titrated separately for breakthrough cancer pain.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Dose-dependent respiratory depression, potentiated by any other sedative on board, remains the single biggest driver of opioid-related perioperative mortality — never underestimate it just because fentanyl feels 'routine.' Rapid high-dose bolus can cause chest wall rigidity severe enough to impair mask ventilation, sometimes needing a muscle relaxant or naloxone to break. Bradycardia, nausea, and pruritus are common. Tolerance and hyperalgesia can develop with prolonged or high-dose infusion. Like all opioids it carries dependence and misuse potential (Schedule II), and accumulation with prolonged infusion means recovery time can be considerably longer than the drug's short single-dose half-life would suggest.

## 19. Morphine (Duramorph / Astramorph) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Natural phenanthrene alkaloid (morphinan skeleton)
- **Tagline**: The original opioid everything else gets compared to — slower, longer-lasting, and genuinely different in renal failure
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Duramorph (morphine sulfate) injection label (Baxter/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for IV analgesia in adults, 2.5–15 mg as a single dose, titrated slowly, repeatable every 3–4 hours as needed. For epidural use, 5 mg initially, with up to 1–2 mg repeat doses after an hour if needed, to a maximum of roughly 10 mg over 24 hours. For intrathecal use, 0.2–1 mg as a single dose (preservative-free formulation only).
- **Stated Off-Label**: Intrathecal and epidural morphine for extended postoperative analgesia after major abdominal, orthopaedic or cardiac surgery, sometimes at doses beyond the label's exact ranges depending on institutional protocol, is common practice. It's also a standard IV PCA agent, and in palliative and cancer pain management it's frequently titrated well beyond acute-pain labelled doses, guided by tolerance and response rather than a fixed ceiling.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Rapid IV bolus can cause histamine-mediated hypotension, flushing and pruritus — give it slowly to blunt this. The renally-cleared active metabolite, morphine-6-glucuronide, is the single biggest reason for caution in renal impairment, where accumulation can cause delayed and genuinely prolonged respiratory depression well after you'd expect the drug to have worn off. With neuraxial administration specifically, delayed respiratory depression from rostral spread of the drug in CSF can occur several hours after dosing — which is exactly why patients who've had neuraxial morphine need extended monitoring, not just the standard recovery-room window. Constipation, urinary retention and pruritus are all more prominent with the neuraxial route. Like every opioid here, it carries tolerance, dependence and misuse potential (Schedule II).

## 20. Hydromorphone (Dilaudid) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Semi-synthetic hydrogenated phenanthrene (morphinan skeleton)
- **Tagline**: Morphine's more potent cousin — often chosen specifically because it lacks morphine's problematic active metabolite
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Dilaudid (hydromorphone hydrochloride) injection label (Purdue Pharma/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for IV analgesia in an opioid-naive adult, 0.2–1 mg IV every 2–3 hours as needed, titrated — opioid-tolerant patients will need meaningfully more. It's also FDA-labelled for IM or subcutaneous injection at the same 0.2–1 mg dose range when IV access isn't available or appropriate.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Neuraxial (epidural or intrathecal) hydromorphone for postoperative or labour analgesia is common practice, as is its use in IV PCA regimens — both sit outside the core parenteral labelling but are well established: epidural, typically 0.5–1 mg as a bolus or a low-concentration infusion; intrathecal, typically 0.05–0.3 mg as a single dose, doses that must never be confused with the far larger IV/IM range above. It's also a standard opioid-rotation choice in cancer and palliative pain management when a patient has troublesome side effects on morphine.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Respiratory depression risk is dose-equivalent to any other mu agonist, but hydromorphone's high potency creates a specific, well-recognised medication-safety hazard: because the effective dose is so much smaller in milligram terms than morphine, a simple ten-fold dosing error (a genuinely common class of medication error) has an outsized, more dangerous consequence than the same error would with a less potent opioid — this is exactly why ISMP and similar safety bodies flag hydromorphone specifically for extra dispensing and labelling caution. Myoclonus and neuroexcitation can occur with very high accumulated doses, particularly in renal impairment. Otherwise, expect the usual opioid picture: nausea, constipation, pruritus, sedation, and the same tolerance, dependence and misuse potential (Schedule II) as every drug in this class.

## 21. Remifentanil (Ultiva) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Esterase-metabolised synthetic phenylpiperidine (ultra-short-acting)
- **Tagline**: An opioid that vanishes within minutes of stopping the infusion — because your own blood and tissue break it down, not your liver
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Ultiva (remifentanil hydrochloride) injection label (originally GlaxoSmithKline/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for induction, given as an infusion alongside an induction agent, 0.5–1 mcg/kg/min. For maintenance of general anaesthesia, 0.0625–2 mcg/kg/min IV, combined with a volatile agent, propofol, or nitrous oxide. For postoperative analgesia in a monitored setting, 0.025–0.2 mcg/kg/min. It isn't given as a simple bolus for postoperative pain — its ultra-short duration and the real risk of acute tolerance and hyperalgesia make that impractical.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's widely used as a sedation/analgesia infusion for spontaneously breathing patients needing dense, rapidly reversible analgesia — awake craniotomy and awake fibreoptic intubation are the classic examples — a well-adopted extension of its labelled maintenance-anaesthesia role. It's also used for patient-controlled obstetric analgesia when neuraxial techniques are contraindicated.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Unlike fentanyl, morphine, hydromorphone or sufentanil, remifentanil is never given epidurally or intrathecally — the commercial formulation contains glycine as an excipient, which is neurotoxic in the subarachnoid/epidural space, so neuraxial administration is specifically contraindicated by the label, not simply unstudied. The single most important practical pitfall otherwise is the flip side of its greatest strength: analgesia vanishes almost the moment the infusion stops, and inadequate planning for that transition is a well-recognised cause of severe, unexpected emergence pain. Opioid-induced hyperalgesia and acute tolerance are more clinically obvious with remifentanil than with most other opioids, especially after high-dose or prolonged intraoperative use. Bradycardia and hypotension are more common than with fentanyl, especially combined with propofol. Chest wall rigidity can occur with rapid bolus or high infusion rates. And genuinely unlike every other opioid here, it needs no dose adjustment for renal or hepatic failure — its esterase-dependent clearance simply doesn't care.

## 22. Sufentanil (Sufenta) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Thienyl-substituted synthetic phenylpiperidine (high potency)
- **Tagline**: One of the most potent opioids in clinical use — small volumes, small margin for dosing error
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Sufenta (sufentanil citrate) injection label (originally Janssen/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved, again deliberately wide to span minor to major surgery: as an adjunct to general anaesthesia, 1–2 mcg/kg IV for maintenance alongside nitrous oxide/oxygen at the low end, up to 2–8 mcg/kg — and as high as 30 mcg/kg for very major procedures like cardiac surgery — as a primary anaesthetic component. For epidural use in some markets/products, small microgram doses (roughly 10–15 mcg) are combined with local anaesthetic for obstetric analgesia.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Intrathecal sufentanil, added to local anaesthetic for labour epidural, combined spinal-epidural, and postoperative analgesia, is extremely common and well-studied practice — technically off-label relative to the primary systemic anaesthesia-adjunct labelling in the US, but a genuine standard of care in obstetric anaesthesia, typically 2.5–10 mcg as a single intrathecal dose in a combined spinal-epidural for labour.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Because it's so potent, chest wall rigidity is a real risk with rapid bolus, and given how small the effective volumes are, it may need neuromuscular blockade to manage airway control if it occurs. Respiratory depression is proportional to its potency — meaning small-volume dosing errors have an outsized effect, so accuracy in drawing it up matters more here than with less potent opioids. Bradycardia occurs, and pruritus is common with neuraxial use. Like every opioid here it carries tolerance, dependence and misuse potential (Schedule II) — and its potency makes diversion or accidental overdose particularly dangerous.

## 23. Alfentanil (Alfenta) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Tetrazole-substituted phenylpiperidine (ultra-rapid onset, low pKa)
- **Tagline**: The fentanyl-family opioid with the fastest peak effect — useful for very short, very painful moments
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Alfenta (alfentanil hydrochloride) injection label (originally Janssen/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for induction, 8–20 mcg/kg IV, with incremental doses of 3–5 mcg/kg for maintenance. For maintenance infusion during general anaesthesia, 0.5–3 mcg/kg/min, titrated. For sedation-analgesia in monitored anaesthesia care, small incremental doses (an initial 3–8 mcg/kg or so), titrated carefully given how fast it acts.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a useful rapid-onset analgesic adjunct for brief, sharply painful moments — line placement, brief endoscopy, ECT — where its very fast peak effect is genuinely advantageous, largely within its broad labelled indications but sometimes used in dosing patterns tailored to procedures shorter than its typical labelled examples.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Chest wall rigidity with rapid bolus is a particular concern given how fast it acts — you can go from normal to rigid before you've fully registered what's happening. Respiratory depression and bradycardia follow the same pattern as other potent opioids. Hepatic impairment or a concurrent potent CYP3A4 inhibitor (certain azole antifungals, some protease inhibitors) can markedly prolong its effect, working against the fast-offset reputation it's known for — worth checking the medication list before assuming it will wear off quickly. Tolerance, dependence and misuse potential (Schedule II) apply as with every opioid here.

## 24. Pethidine (Meperidine) (Demerol) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Full mu-opioid agonist • Synthetic phenylpiperidine (anticholinergic, active normeperidine metabolite)
- **Tagline**: The opioid nobody starts on anymore — kept alive mainly for treating shivering, avoided for everything else
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Demerol (meperidine hydrochloride) injection label (Sanofi/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for analgesia, 50–150 mg IV/IM/SC every 3–4 hours as needed, or the same range orally, though oral use is markedly less reliable given significant first-pass metabolism. For treating postoperative shivering specifically — a distinct, much smaller dose from its analgesic use — 12.5–25 mg IV is the standard teaching dose.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Its most durable modern niche is exactly that shivering treatment, now arguably its primary reason for staying on the shelf in many units. It was historically favoured for renal or biliary colic on the theory that it causes less sphincter of Oddi spasm than morphine, though contemporary evidence for a real clinical difference is more equivocal than the traditional teaching suggests.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Normeperidine accumulation is the defining hazard — tremor, myoclonus, and seizures can follow repeated dosing or use in renal impairment, and normeperidine toxicity isn't reversed by naloxone the way the parent drug's opioid effects are, since normeperidine's excitatory effects aren't opioid-receptor-mediated. It's absolutely contraindicated with MAOIs (current or within the past two weeks) — the combination can trigger a severe, sometimes fatal serotonergic crisis, a drug interaction worth knowing cold rather than looking up under pressure. Its anticholinergic activity brings tachycardia, dry mouth and mild mydriasis rather than the miosis typical of other opioids. Because of the normeperidine risk specifically, many hospital formularies now restrict pethidine to short-course or shivering use rather than routine analgesia.

## 25. Tramadol (Ultram) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Atypical / Weak mu-opioid agonist • Dual-mechanism synthetic cyclohexanol (central SNRI / monoamine uptake inhibitor)
- **Tagline**: A weak opioid with a second, independent mechanism bolted on — which is exactly what makes it awkward with antidepressants
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Ultram (tramadol hydrochloride) tablet label (Janssen/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved (oral only in the US — no IV formulation is approved domestically, though parenteral tramadol is used in some other countries): 50–100 mg orally every 4–6 hours as needed, maximum 400 mg/day (300 mg/day in patients over 75). Extended-release formulations are dosed once daily for chronic pain. A combination product with paracetamol (Ultracet) is separately FDA-approved at lower per-dose tramadol content.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's used off-label for premature ejaculation, exploiting its serotonergic component rather than its analgesic one. It's also sometimes chosen specifically for its comparatively lower respiratory depression and abuse-potential profile relative to full mu agonists at equianalgesic acute-pain doses, though 'lower' is not 'absent,' and its own distinct risks (seizures, serotonin syndrome) need equal respect.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Seizures are the headline risk, dose-dependent and meaningfully more likely with concurrent SSRIs, SNRIs, TCAs, or other drugs that lower seizure threshold — worth checking the medication list before prescribing. Serotonin syndrome is a real risk with other serotonergic drugs, and it's contraindicated with MAOIs for the same reason. Withdrawal on stopping can show an atypical mixed picture — both classic opioid-withdrawal features and SNRI-discontinuation-type symptoms (anxiety, panic, paraesthesia) — which can catch clinicians off guard if they're only watching for the opioid pattern. Hypoglycaemia has been reported, more often early in treatment. It's a Schedule IV controlled substance — real but lower misuse potential than a full mu agonist.

## 26. Buprenorphine (Buprenex (injectable) / Suboxone, Subutex (sublingual) / Butrans (patch) / Belbuca (buccal)) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Partial mu-opioid agonist / Kappa antagonist • Semi-synthetic thebaine-derived oripavine (ceiling effect on respiratory depression)
- **Tagline**: So tightly bound to the mu receptor that it's hard to displace either way — a genuine ceiling on overdose risk, and a genuine headache if you need to reverse it
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Buprenex (buprenorphine hydrochloride) injection label; Suboxone/Subutex, Butrans and Belbuca labels for the respective non-parenteral products (Indivior/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved, and deliberately spread across several distinct products for different indications: for acute pain (Buprenex), 0.3 mg IM or slow IV every 6–8 hours. For opioid use disorder, sublingual buprenorphine (alone, or combined with naloxone as Suboxone to deter injection misuse) is induced at roughly 2–4 mg SL and titrated to a typical maintenance range of 8–24 mg/day, only once the patient is in mild-to-moderate withdrawal from their prior opioid to avoid precipitating a worse one. For chronic pain, transdermal Butrans patches deliver 5–20 mcg/hour continuously over 7 days, and buccal Belbuca is dosed in micrograms every 12 hours.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Its ceiling effect on respiratory depression makes it an increasingly discussed option for acute perioperative pain management in patients already on it for opioid use disorder or chronic pain, an evolving area of practice rather than a settled protocol — planning typically involves close coordination between anaesthesia and addiction medicine rather than simply stopping it preoperatively, which risks withdrawal and poorly controlled pain.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Precipitated withdrawal is the classic pitfall when starting buprenorphine too soon after a full agonist — timing the first dose to genuine early withdrawal, not just 'a few hours since the last dose,' is what avoids it. Respiratory depression still occurs despite the ceiling effect, and becomes a real, well-documented risk again when combined with benzodiazepines or other CNS depressants — a combination the FDA specifically warns about. Its very high receptor affinity means standard-dose naloxone may not fully reverse it — a genuine reversal significantly higher than usual, or repeated dosing, may be needed if toxicity occurs. Hepatotoxicity is uncommon but reported, and QT prolongation has been described at higher doses of some formulations.

## 27. Nalbuphine (Nubain) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Mixed kappa agonist / Mu partial antagonist • Semi-synthetic 14-hydroxymorphinan
- **Tagline**: A kappa agonist/mu antagonist combination best known for treating the itch that morphine and fentanyl cause, not for treating pain
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Nubain (nalbuphine hydrochloride) injection label (Hikma/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for analgesia, 10 mg IV, IM or SC every 3–4 hours as needed (up to 20 mg per dose, maximum 160 mg/day), the same dose whichever of those three routes is used. For treating opioid-induced pruritus specifically — a much smaller, off-label dose than the analgesic range above — 2.5–5 mg IV titrated in small increments is standard teaching.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Treating pruritus from neuraxial or systemic mu-agonist opioids, and partially reversing other mu-mediated side effects while preserving some analgesia, are both well-established off-label uses built directly on its mixed-receptor pharmacology.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Sedation is more prominent than with an equianalgesic dose of a pure mu agonist. The ceiling effect blunts but doesn't eliminate respiratory depression risk, particularly combined with other sedatives. Giving it to an opioid-tolerant patient already on a full mu agonist can precipitate withdrawal, exactly as with any agonist-antagonist. One genuinely distinctive, exam-relevant fact: nalbuphine is not a DEA-scheduled controlled substance in the US, unlike essentially every other opioid on this list — a real practical difference in how it's stored, prescribed and tracked.

## 28. Pentazocine (Talwin (also Talwin NX, combined with naloxone)) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Mixed kappa agonist / Weak mu antagonist • Synthetic benzomorphan
- **Tagline**: One of the first agonist-antagonist opioids — largely retired now, remembered for causing dysphoria rather than euphoria
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Talwin (pentazocine) injection and Talwin NX (pentazocine/naloxone) tablet labels (Sanofi/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: orally, 50 mg every 3–4 hours as needed (as Talwin NX, combined with naloxone specifically to deter dissolving and injecting the tablets — the oral naloxone component isn't bioavailable enough to blunt oral analgesia, but becomes active and precipitates withdrawal if injected). Parenterally (IM, SC or IV), 30 mg every 3–4 hours, to a maximum of roughly 360 mg/day.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's now rarely chosen as first-line therapy for anything, largely supplanted by agents with a cleaner side-effect profile — its remaining use is mostly where an agonist-antagonist's ceiling effect is specifically wanted and its psychotomimetic tendency is judged an acceptable trade-off.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Dysphoria and hallucinations, kappa-mediated, are its most distinctive complication and the main reason it fell out of favour. Repeated subcutaneous or intramuscular injection has historically been linked to severe local tissue damage — sclerosis, fibrosis, occasionally ulceration — particularly with chronic misuse of the injectable form. It raises cardiac workload more than most opioids, worth remembering in significant coronary or valvular disease. It's a Schedule IV controlled substance, and can precipitate withdrawal in opioid-tolerant patients like any agonist-antagonist.

## 29. Naloxone (Narcan) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Pure competitive opioid receptor antagonist • N-allyl substituted morphinan (mu, kappa, and delta blocker)
- **Tagline**: The opioid antidote — pure antagonism, fast onset, and a duration deliberately shorter than most of the drugs it's reversing
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Narcan (naloxone hydrochloride) injection and Narcan Nasal Spray labels (Emergent BioSolutions/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for IV use, 0.4–2 mg, repeated every 2–3 minutes as needed, titrated in much smaller increments (0.04–0.1 mg) in anyone opioid-dependent or on chronic opioid therapy, specifically to avoid precipitating a severe, painful withdrawal reaction. The same dose range applies IM or SC when IV access isn't available. Intranasal naloxone (Narcan Nasal Spray) is FDA-approved as a 4 mg (or a higher-strength 8 mg) spray in one nostril, repeatable in the other nostril if there's no response — the formulation behind most community and take-home naloxone programmes. For prolonged reversal after a long-acting or high-dose opioid, a continuous infusion (roughly two-thirds of the effective bolus dose per hour) is used, titrated to effect.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's used diagnostically in undifferentiated altered consciousness where opioid toxicity is a possibility, and is the basis of essentially all bystander/community opioid-overdose-response programmes via the intranasal or auto-injector routes.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Precipitated acute withdrawal — agitation, tachycardia, hypertension, vomiting, and in a patient with significant pain, an abrupt return of severe pain — is the most common real-world problem, and is exactly why titrated small doses are used in opioid-tolerant patients rather than a full reversal dose. Renarcotisation from its comparatively short half-life demands ongoing monitoring, not a single dose and discharge, especially after a long-acting opioid or a large overdose. Pulmonary oedema has been reported, uncommonly, particularly with rapid full-dose reversal. Cardiac arrhythmias are rarely reported too.

## 30. Naltrexone (ReVia (oral) / Vivitrol (monthly IM depot)) — [Category: OPIOIDS]
- **Classification**: Pure competitive opioid receptor antagonist • Cyclopropylmethyl substituted morphinan (long-acting orally bioavailable blocker)
- **Tagline**: Naloxone's longer-acting, orally active cousin — built for sustained blockade, not emergency reversal
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — ReVia (naltrexone hydrochloride) tablet and Vivitrol (naltrexone extended-release injectable suspension) labels (originally Duramed/Alkermes, generic available); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: orally, 50 mg once daily for maintenance treatment of opioid use disorder or alcohol use disorder, only after confirming an adequate opioid-free interval (typically at least 7–10 days, sometimes assessed with a naloxone challenge test first) to avoid precipitating withdrawal. As a monthly IM depot (Vivitrol, into the gluteal muscle), 380 mg once every 4 weeks — genuinely one of the few opioid-class drugs given by scheduled intramuscular injection rather than as-needed dosing.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Low-dose naltrexone (roughly 1–4.5 mg, well below the standard blocking dose) is used off-label for chronic pain, fibromyalgia and some autoimmune conditions, on the theory that at that dose it modulates glial and immune signalling rather than simply blocking opioid receptors — an active but still evolving area of evidence, not yet a settled indication.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Precipitated withdrawal is the major early risk if it's started too soon after an opioid — confirming the opioid-free interval matters more here than with naloxone, since naltrexone's effect lasts so much longer once it's on board. Hepatotoxicity is a labelled concern at doses above the approved range, though it's been less clearly demonstrated at standard therapeutic doses. Its most important perioperative implication is what it does to FUTURE opioid use: a patient established on naltrexone who then needs acute pain control — trauma, emergency surgery — can be genuinely difficult to treat with standard opioid doses, and this needs proactive planning with the anaesthesia and pain teams rather than being discovered mid-case.

## 31. Ketorolac (Toradol) — [Category: NSAIDS]
- **Classification**: Non-selective NSAID • Pyrrolo-pyrrole / Acetic acid derivative (potent COX-1 > COX-2 inhibitor)
- **Tagline**: An NSAID potent enough to substitute for opioids in acute pain — with a strict 5-day clock attached
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Toradol (ketorolac tromethamine) injection label (Roche/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for adults under 65 with normal renal function, 30 mg IV or 60 mg IM as a single dose, or 15–30 mg IV/IM every 6 hours, to a maximum of 120 mg/day — and critically, for no more than 5 days total combined duration across all routes. For adults 65 or older, anyone with renal impairment, or under 50 kg, the dose drops to 15 mg IV/IM, maximum 60 mg/day. A separate intranasal product (Sprix) is also FDA-approved: 15.75 mg (one spray) in each nostril every 6–8 hours (31.5 mg total per dose) for adults under 65, halved to one spray in only one nostril for those 65 or older, under 50 kg, or with renal impairment — the same 120 mg/60 mg daily ceilings and 5-day maximum apply across whichever routes are combined. It isn't approved for epidural or intrathecal use, and the label specifically advises against it as a neuraxial analgesic adjunct.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Use beyond the labelled 5-day maximum is occasionally seen in select chronic-pain contexts under close monitoring, but this isn't standard practice and is generally discouraged given the cumulative GI and renal risk. Paediatric postoperative analgesia protocols commonly use it at weight-based doses below the primary adult labelling, a widespread and reasonable practice that nonetheless sits outside the core adult label.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The label carries black-box warnings worth taking seriously: GI bleeding, ulceration and perforation risk, which rises with dose, duration beyond 5 days, age, concurrent anticoagulants, corticosteroids or other NSAIDs, or any prior peptic ulcer history; and real perioperative bleeding risk from platelet inhibition, which is why it's contraindicated with active bleeding, before major surgery where haemostasis really matters, and in anyone on anticoagulants. It's nephrotoxic through reduced renal prostaglandin-mediated perfusion, particularly dangerous in hypovolaemia or with other nephrotoxins on board. It's contraindicated in labour and delivery and in nursing mothers, and in advanced renal impairment. There's a class-wide cardiovascular thrombotic risk warning too, though the evidence specifically for short-course ketorolac is more limited than for chronic NSAID use. And in aspirin- or NSAID-sensitive asthmatics, it can trigger bronchospasm or an anaphylactoid reaction.

## 32. Ibuprofen (Motrin / Advil (IV: Caldolor)) — [Category: NSAIDS]
- **Classification**: Non-selective NSAID • Propionic acid derivative (reversible COX-1 and COX-2 inhibitor)
- **Tagline**: The familiar over-the-counter NSAID, now also available IV as a genuine multimodal analgesia component
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Caldolor (ibuprofen) injection label; Motrin/generic ibuprofen oral label (Cumberland Pharmaceuticals (IV)/generic (oral)); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: orally, 400–800 mg every 6 hours as needed in adults, up to a prescription maximum of 3200 mg/day (over-the-counter labelling caps this lower, at 1200 mg/day). IV (Caldolor), for pain: 400–800 mg every 6 hours as needed, infused over at least 30 minutes, maximum 3200 mg/day; for fever, an initial 400 mg then 400 mg every 4–6 hours, or 100–200 mg every 4 hours.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a common component of scheduled, opioid-sparing multimodal postoperative analgesia regimens — often alternated or combined with paracetamol at standard labelled doses, though the specific fixed-combination regimen itself hasn't been individually studied and approved by the FDA as a unit. Weight-based paediatric perioperative dosing follows paediatric-specific references rather than the adult label directly.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: GI ulceration and bleeding risk exists but is generally lower than ketorolac's at equivalent short-term use, still rising with dose, duration, age, and concurrent anticoagulants. Renal impairment risk is real, particularly with hypovolaemia or concurrent nephrotoxic drugs. There's a class-wide cardiovascular thrombotic risk, carried as a boxed warning on the label. Platelet inhibition increases perioperative bleeding risk, similarly to other non-selective NSAIDs. It can provoke bronchospasm in aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease, and can cause fluid retention and worsen blood pressure control.

## 33. Diclofenac (Voltaren) — [Category: NSAIDS]
- **Classification**: Non-selective NSAID • Phenylacetic acid derivative (balanced COX-1 / COX-2 inhibitor)
- **Tagline**: An NSAID available in almost every route imaginable — oral, topical, ophthalmic, and IV
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Dyloject (diclofenac sodium) injection label; Voltaren/generic diclofenac oral and topical labels (Hikma (IV)/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: orally (immediate-release), 50 mg two to three times daily, maximum 150 mg/day, for pain and inflammatory conditions like osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. IV (Dyloject), 37.5 mg every 6 hours as needed for acute moderate-to-severe pain, maximum 150 mg/day, infused over just 15 seconds. Topical gel and patch formulations are dosed per their specific product labelling for localised musculoskeletal pain.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's used as part of multimodal perioperative analgesia alongside paracetamol and opioids at standard labelled doses — a widely adopted combination in enhanced-recovery pathways that hasn't itself been specifically studied and approved as a fixed regimen by the FDA. Extended perioperative courses beyond typical short-term acute-pain use are also seen in some enhanced-recovery protocols.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The class-wide boxed warning applies in full — cardiovascular thrombotic events (MI, stroke) and serious, potentially fatal GI bleeding, ulceration or perforation, both risks rising with dose and duration. Diclofenac carries a genuinely higher signal for clinically significant hepatotoxicity than most other NSAIDs — worth actively monitoring liver function with any prolonged course, not just assuming it's interchangeable with ibuprofen in this respect. Renal impairment, platelet inhibition and bleeding risk, hypersensitivity and bronchospasm in NSAID-sensitive patients, and fluid retention with blood pressure elevation all apply as with the rest of the class.

## 34. Celecoxib (Celebrex) — [Category: NSAIDS]
- **Classification**: Selective COX-2 inhibitor • Diaryl-substituted pyrazole (platelet-sparing coxib)
- **Tagline**: The NSAID that spares platelets — genuinely useful when bleeding risk is the deciding factor
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Celebrex (celecoxib) capsule label (Pfizer/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for osteoarthritis, 200 mg once daily or 100 mg twice daily. For rheumatoid arthritis, 100–200 mg twice daily. For acute pain in adults, an initial 400 mg dose, then 200 mg on day 1 if needed, followed by 200 mg twice daily as needed thereafter.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a common component of multimodal perioperative analgesia, given pre- or postoperatively, specifically chosen when minimal antiplatelet effect matters — surgery with a genuine bleeding concern — a widely adopted enhanced-recovery-pathway practice that sits outside the arthritis/acute-pain framing the original label was built around.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: It carries the same class-wide boxed warning for cardiovascular thrombotic risk as every NSAID, and this risk may be dose- and duration-dependent — it's the same underlying concern that led to the market withdrawal of related COX-2-selective drugs rofecoxib and valdecoxib. GI bleeding and ulceration risk is lower than with non-selective NSAIDs but not zero, and importantly, giving it alongside aspirin largely erases the GI advantage, since aspirin's own COX-1 effect is still fully present. Renal risk isn't eliminated by COX-1 sparing, since COX-2 also contributes to renal prostaglandin synthesis. Sulfonamide-related hypersensitivity reactions can occur. It causes fluid retention and hypertension like other NSAIDs, and the label specifically contraindicates it in the setting of perioperative pain around coronary artery bypass graft surgery.

## 35. Paracetamol (Acetaminophen) (Tylenol / Ofirmev (IV)) — [Category: NSAIDS]
- **Classification**: Para-aminophenol derivative • Central COX-3 / peroxidase inhibitor & indirect TRPA1 modulator
- **Tagline**: Not an NSAID at all — the one analgesic on this list with essentially no bleeding, GI or renal downside
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Ofirmev (acetaminophen) injection label; Tylenol/generic acetaminophen oral label (Mallinckrodt (IV)/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for adults and adolescents 50 kg and over, 650–1000 mg every 4–6 hours as needed, orally or IV, to a maximum of 4000 mg/day — though many institutions and more cautious current guidance favour a lower 3000 mg/day ceiling, particularly with any hepatic risk factor present. IV doses are infused over 15 minutes. For patients under 50 kg, and in paediatrics, weight-based dosing applies per the IV label — 15 mg/kg every 6 hours, maximum 75 mg/kg/day, never exceeding the adult maximum.
- **Stated Off-Label**: There's essentially no meaningful off-label use to speak of here — its role as a scheduled (not just as-needed) component of multimodal, opioid-sparing perioperative and enhanced-recovery analgesia protocols is entirely within its labelled analgesic indication, even though 'give it on a fixed schedule rather than PRN' is more a practice pattern than a distinct label claim.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The IV formulation carries a boxed warning for severe hepatotoxicity, almost always from exceeding the maximum daily dose — a risk that's meaningfully higher with pre-existing liver disease, chronic alcohol use, malnutrition, or simple dosing errors from combining multiple acetaminophen-containing products without realising it. Rare but serious hypersensitivity and skin reactions, including Stevens-Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, are a labelled warning. Its biggest practical safety point is one of arithmetic, not pharmacology: always reconcile the total daily acetaminophen dose across every formulation a patient is receiving — oral, IV, and any combination opioid-acetaminophen product — since inadvertent overdose from simply not adding these up correctly is a genuinely common and entirely preventable error. Otherwise, its safety profile is its biggest selling point: minimal cardiovascular, renal, GI or platelet effect compared with NSAIDs.

## 36. Phenylephrine (Neo-Synephrine (IV: Vazculep)) — [Category: VASOPRESSORS]
- **Classification**: Direct sympathomimetic • Pure selective alpha-1 adrenergic agonist
- **Tagline**: A pure vasoconstrictor with no direct effect on the heart — which is exactly why it's the obstetric anaesthetist's default pressor
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Vazculep (phenylephrine hydrochloride) injection label (Eagle Pharmaceuticals/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved (Vazculep): for a bolus dose of hypotension, 0.1–0.5 mg IV, repeatable every 10–15 minutes as needed. As a continuous infusion, an initial rate of 0.1–0.18 mg/min, titrated to target blood pressure, typically then reduced to a lower maintenance range once stable.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's the preferred first-line vasopressor for spinal-anaesthesia-induced hypotension in obstetric anaesthesia — supported explicitly by obstetric anaesthesia society guidance, which favours it over ephedrine specifically because it's associated with less fetal acidosis. A low-dose infusion is routinely used to maintain uteroplacental perfusion during caesarean delivery under neuraxial anaesthesia — a well-established practice that extends somewhat beyond the drug's original general-hypotension labelling.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Reflex bradycardia can be pronounced, occasionally needing an antimuscarinic or a dose reduction to manage. Because it's a pure vasoconstrictor with no inotropic support, overdosing it can reduce cardiac output and organ perfusion — including uteroplacental and splanchnic flow — through excessive afterload combined with reflex bradycardia, rather than genuinely helping the circulation. Extravasation causes tissue necrosis, as with any potent vasoconstrictor. It's relatively contraindicated as a sole agent in severe bradycardia, or wherever a large increase in afterload would be poorly tolerated, such as significant left ventricular failure.

## 37. Norepinephrine (Noradrenaline) (Levophed) — [Category: VASOPRESSORS]
- **Classification**: Direct sympathomimetic • Potent alpha-1 and beta-1 adrenergic agonist
- **Tagline**: The default first-line vasopressor in septic and most distributive shock — raises pressure without much collateral tachycardia
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Levophed (norepinephrine bitartrate) injection label (Hospira/Pfizer); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: as a continuous IV infusion, starting at 0.5–1 mcg/min and titrated to a target mean arterial pressure — commonly at least 65 mmHg in septic shock, per Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance — with typical maintenance ranges of 2–20 mcg/min or higher in refractory shock, since the label itself gives broad titration guidance rather than a fixed ceiling.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Peripheral administration — through a large, well-sited proximal IV rather than strictly requiring a central line — for a limited duration at low-to-moderate doses is increasingly supported by emergency and critical-care literature, and is used as an accepted deviation from the traditional central-line-only teaching, particularly while central access is still being obtained.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Extravasation causes real tissue ischaemia and necrosis, since it's such a potent vasoconstrictor — phentolamine infiltration is the standard antidote if it happens. Overdosing it relative to the degree of shock present can reduce perfusion to the extremities, skin and splanchnic or renal beds through excessive vasoconstriction. Arrhythmias are less common than with epinephrine or dopamine but can still occur, especially with pre-existing ischaemic heart disease. Reflex bradycardia is uncommon but possible. Most institutional protocols still call for central venous access for prolonged or high-dose infusion, given the extravasation risk with peripheral administration over time.

## 38. Epinephrine (Adrenaline) (Adrenalin) — [Category: VASOPRESSORS]
- **Classification**: Direct sympathomimetic • Non-selective alpha and beta adrenergic agonist
- **Tagline**: The one drug that works at every dose and every route — anaphylaxis, cardiac arrest, and everything unstable in between
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Adrenalin (epinephrine) injection label (Par Pharmaceutical/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for anaphylaxis, 0.3–0.5 mg IM (the 1 mg/mL, '1:1000' concentration), repeatable every 5–15 minutes as needed; for severe or refractory cases with cardiovascular collapse, titrated IV boluses (50–100 mcg increments of the 0.1 mg/mL, '1:10,000' concentration) or an infusion in a monitored setting. For cardiac arrest under ACLS, 1 mg IV/IO every 3–5 minutes. As an adjunct for cardiogenic or vasodilatory shock, a continuous infusion typically 0.01–0.5 mcg/kg/min, titrated to effect.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Nebulised epinephrine — typically 0.5 mL of 2.25% racemic epinephrine, or 3–5 mL of the 1 mg/mL ('1:1000') formulation, diluted in saline — is well-established, though technically off-label, treatment for post-extubation or croup-related upper-airway swelling and stridor, exploiting its alpha-mediated mucosal vasoconstriction. Low-dose diluted 'push-dose' epinephrine (5–20 mcg increments) for acute perioperative hypotension or bradycardia is increasingly used in anaesthesia practice, sitting outside the primary anaphylaxis/arrest labelling framework.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Tachyarrhythmias, including ventricular arrhythmias, and hypertension are real risks, historically worsened by myocardial sensitisation from older volatile agents like halothane (less of a concern with modern agents) — at excessive doses this can precipitate myocardial ischaemia or even intracranial haemorrhage. Extravasation causes tissue necrosis. Anxiety, tremor and headache are common at therapeutic doses. It can cause pulmonary oedema in a vulnerable heart through the afterload it generates. High-dose or prolonged infusion can cause a beta-2-mediated lactic acidosis. And a genuinely important, well-documented safety hazard is accidental concentration confusion between the 1 mg/mL and 0.1 mg/mL formulations — a mix-up that has caused serious harm often enough that both the FDA and ISMP have issued specific safety communications about it, so always double-check which concentration you're actually holding.

## 39. Vasopressin (Arginine Vasopressin) (Vasostrict) — [Category: VASOPRESSORS]
- **Classification**: Non-adrenergic peptide hormone • Vascular V1a and renal V2 receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: A non-catecholamine pressor for the shock that's stopped responding to catecholamines
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Vasostrict (vasopressin) injection label (Par Pharmaceutical/Endo); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for septic shock, a continuous IV infusion of 0.01–0.07 units/min, titrated — though in practice it's commonly used as a fixed low dose, around 0.03–0.04 units/min, as an adjunct layered on top of norepinephrine, per Surviving Sepsis Campaign practice, rather than aggressively titrated up like a first-line pressor.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a common adjunct vasopressor in refractory vasoplegic shock after cardiac surgery or cardiopulmonary bypass. It was historically used as an adjunct or alternative to epinephrine in some earlier ACLS cardiac arrest algorithms, though later ACLS updates have de-emphasised that role in favour of epinephrine alone. It's also used more broadly for catecholamine-refractory hypotension in various ICU vasoplegic states beyond septic shock specifically.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Excessive vasoconstriction can cause digital, splanchnic or coronary ischaemia — a real concern in anyone with pre-existing vascular disease. Prolonged use can cause hyponatraemia and water retention through its V2-mediated antidiuretic effect. It can reduce cardiac output if a failing ventricle can't tolerate the added afterload. And unlike norepinephrine, it isn't typically titrated up aggressively for further pressor effect once at its usual adjunct dose — it's used as a fixed-dose add-on rather than the primary titrated agent in most septic shock protocols.

## 40. Dopamine (Intropin) — [Category: VASOPRESSORS]
- **Classification**: Direct & indirect sympathomimetic • Dose-dependent D1/D2, beta-1, and alpha-1 agonist
- **Tagline**: Once the textbook first-line pressor, now mostly second-line — the dose-dependent receptor story is famous, and famously oversimplified
- **Source Citation**: UpToDate and Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed. — generic dopamine hydrochloride injection (brand Intropin discontinued; originally DuPont).
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: as a continuous IV infusion, typically starting at 2–5 mcg/kg/min and titrated by haemodynamic response, up to 20–50 mcg/kg/min in refractory cases per the label's broad range — though most current guideline-directed practice favours norepinephrine as first-line, reserving dopamine for select scenarios like bradycardia-associated hypotension.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's used for symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine, as a temporising chronotropic and pressor infusion while preparing for pacing — an ACLS-recognised use consistent with its broad labelling. Its older use for 'renal protection' in early acute kidney injury or oliguria is now considered outdated, unsupported by contemporary evidence, and generally discouraged.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: It's more arrhythmogenic than norepinephrine at equivalent pressor doses — several trials have shown higher arrhythmia rates with dopamine compared with norepinephrine in septic shock, which is exactly why it's been relegated to second-line status behind norepinephrine in current Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance. Extravasation causes tissue necrosis, like any potent vasoconstrictor. It raises myocardial oxygen demand, with real ischaemia risk. Nausea and vomiting are common. And with prolonged infusion it can suppress anterior pituitary hormone release, including prolactin and TSH — a lesser-known but genuine effect worth knowing exists, even if it rarely changes acute management.

## 41. Dobutamine (Dobutrex) — [Category: VASOPRESSORS]
- **Classification**: Direct sympathomimetic • Selective beta-1 adrenergic inotrope (inodilator)
- **Tagline**: An inotrope, not a vasopressor — it boosts the heart's output without meaningfully raising blood pressure
- **Source Citation**: UpToDate and Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed. — generic dobutamine injection (brand Dobutrex discontinued; originally Eli Lilly).
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: as a continuous IV infusion, 2.5–15 mcg/kg/min, titrated to haemodynamic and clinical response, up to 20 mcg/kg/min in some protocols per the upper end of the label's titration range, for short-term management of cardiac decompensation — cardiogenic shock, acute decompensated heart failure with low output.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's the standard pharmacologic stress agent for dobutamine stress echocardiography — a well-established diagnostic use, though formally distinct from its haemodynamic-support labelling. It's also used as an adjunct inotrope in septic shock where myocardial dysfunction and low cardiac output persist despite adequate fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy, per Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidance.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Tachyarrhythmias and increased myocardial oxygen demand are real risks, and can precipitate or worsen ischaemia in a patient with coronary artery disease. Hypotension can occur, largely from its mild beta-2-mediated vasodilation, especially in a hypovolaemic patient — optimise volume status before starting it. Tachyphylaxis develops with prolonged infusion, through beta-receptor downregulation. Significant tachycardia or arrhythmia can be provoked during stress-echo use, which is exactly why that test needs cardiac monitoring and readiness to treat. And it's worth being explicit about what it isn't: dobutamine is not a vasopressor, and shouldn't be relied on alone to raise blood pressure in a genuinely hypotensive shock state without adequate perfusion-pressure support from a true pressor alongside it.

## 42. Lidocaine (Xylocaine) — [Category: LOCAL]
- **Classification**: Aminoamide • Intermediate-acting local anaesthetic (fast onset, hepatic CYP1A2/3A4)
- **Tagline**: The prototype amide local anaesthetic — the one every other local gets compared to, and a drug in its own right IV
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Xylocaine (lidocaine hydrochloride) injection label (Fresenius Kabi/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for infiltration or peripheral nerve block, dosing varies by block and concentration (0.5–2% solutions), with a widely used maximum single dose of roughly 4.5 mg/kg plain (up to about 300 mg) or 7 mg/kg with epinephrine added (up to about 500 mg), per standard local anaesthetic dosing guidance. For epidural anaesthesia, 1.5–2% solutions are typical, with volume and level determined by the block needed. For spinal (intrathecal) anaesthesia, hyperbaric 5% lidocaine in 7.5% dextrose (Xylocaine-MPF Spinal) is FDA-labelled at 1–1.5 mL (50–75 mg), though this concentration is used less often now given the transient neurologic symptom risk noted under Complications. As an IV antiarrhythmic for ventricular arrhythmia, 1–1.5 mg/kg IV bolus, repeatable, followed by a 1–4 mg/min infusion.
- **Stated Off-Label**: An IV lidocaine infusion is a well-studied, widely adopted component of multimodal perioperative analgesia, particularly in abdominal surgery — several trials show it reduces opioid consumption, pain scores, and speeds return of bowel function, making it a genuine ERAS-pathway tool even though it's off-label relative to its approved local-anaesthetic and antiarrhythmic indications. Topical or nebulised lidocaine is also used to suppress cough during awake airway procedures. It's also the standard agent for IV regional anaesthesia (Bier block) — 0.5% plain lidocaine, roughly 3 mg/kg (about 40–50 mL in an average adult arm), a well-established technique that nonetheless sits outside the core label's enumerated routes.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) from inadvertent intravascular injection or simply too large a total dose is the complication to know cold for this entire drug class — early CNS signs (perioral numbness, tinnitus, a metallic taste, agitation) progress to seizures, then cardiovascular signs (arrhythmia, conduction block, collapse), managed following the ASRA LAST checklist: airway management, seizure control, and IV lipid emulsion ('lipid rescue') if cardiovascular toxicity develops. Methaemoglobinaemia is more classically linked to prilocaine and benzocaine, but has been reported with lidocaine too. High-concentration intrathecal lidocaine (historically 5% hyperbaric) has been linked to transient neurologic symptoms, which is part of why it's now used less for spinal anaesthesia than it once was. Genuine allergic reactions are rare with amide local anaesthetics (far more common with esters, related to their PABA metabolite), but can still occur, sometimes tied to preservatives in the formulation rather than the drug itself.

## 43. Bupivacaine (Marcaine / Sensorcaine (liposomal: Exparel)) — [Category: LOCAL]
- **Classification**: Aminoamide • Long-acting local anaesthetic (high protein binding, cardiotoxicity risk)
- **Tagline**: Long-acting and high-potency — and the local anaesthetic most likely to stop a heart if it gets into a vein by accident
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Marcaine (bupivacaine hydrochloride) injection label; Exparel (bupivacaine liposome injectable suspension) label (Pfizer (Marcaine)/Pacira (Exparel)); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved (Marcaine, standard solution): for epidural, peripheral nerve block or infiltration, concentration and volume vary by block and indication (0.25–0.75% solutions), with a commonly cited maximum single dose of roughly 2.5–3 mg/kg, consistent with cumulative local anaesthetic toxicity thresholds referenced in the label and toxicology literature. For spinal (intrathecal) anaesthesia, hyperbaric 0.75% bupivacaine in 8.25% dextrose (Marcaine Spinal) is FDA-labelled — typically 1–1.5 mL (7.5–11.25 mg) for a lower/saddle block, up to about 2 mL (15 mg) for the higher level needed for caesarean delivery, titrated to the block height required; the FDA label specifically warns against using the plain 0.75% concentration for obstetric epidural anaesthesia — see Complications. Exparel (liposomal) is dosed up to 266 mg (20 mL of 1.3%) infiltrated into the surgical site for postsurgical analgesia, per its own specific label — always check the current label for its exact approved routes, since these have expanded over time to include certain peripheral nerve blocks beyond the original surgical-site-infiltration indication.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Lower-concentration plain bupivacaine (roughly 0.0625–0.125%) is standard practice for labour epidural analgesia, chosen specifically to preserve motor function while still providing good sensory analgesia — a well-established technique built on the drug's dose-dependent differential block.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity carries a disproportionately severe cardiotoxic signature with bupivacaine compared with other local anaesthetics — ventricular arrhythmias and cardiac arrest can appear abruptly and can be genuinely refractory to standard ACLS without prompt IV lipid emulsion therapy, a fact that directly drove the widespread adoption of lipid rescue protocols in the first place. The FDA label specifically warns against using the 0.75% concentration in obstetric epidural anaesthesia, following reports of cardiac arrest after accidental intravascular injection at that particular concentration. Chondrolysis has been reported with continuous intra-articular infusion, which the label specifically warns against. Methaemoglobinaemia is not a typical bupivacaine effect — that belongs more to the esters and to prilocaine.

## 44. Ropivacaine (Naropin) — [Category: LOCAL]
- **Classification**: Aminoamide • Long-acting local anaesthetic (pure S-enantiomer, differential sensory block)
- **Tagline**: Built as a deliberately safer bupivacaine — a single enantiomer with a genuinely wider cardiac safety margin
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Naropin (ropivacaine hydrochloride) injection label (originally AstraZeneca/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for epidural surgical anaesthesia, 0.5–1% solutions, with the dose depending on the block level required. For epidural labour or postoperative analgesia, 0.2% is standard, typically as an infusion (roughly 6–14 mL/h per label-referenced ranges) or intermittent bolus. For peripheral nerve block or infiltration, 0.2–0.75% depending on the indication and how dense a block is needed. Maximum recommended single dose is procedure- and concentration-specific — generally referenced around 3–4 mg/kg for major blocks, but always verify against the live label and your institutional protocol for the specific block you're performing.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Continuous peripheral nerve catheter infusions for outpatient postoperative analgesia, at low concentrations (roughly 0.1–0.2%), extend somewhat beyond some of the originally studied dosing scenarios but are widely supported in the regional anaesthesia literature and are broadly consistent with its labelled peripheral-block use. Intrathecal ropivacaine, though not FDA-labelled for that route in the US, is used off-label — more established outside the US — for spinal anaesthesia at roughly 15–25 mg (3–5 mL of 0.5%), generally needing a higher milligram dose than bupivacaine for an equivalent block.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity remains possible, though the threshold and severity for cardiotoxicity are generally more favourable than bupivacaine's at equivalent doses — still managed with the same ASRA LAST protocol, lipid emulsion included, if cardiovascular toxicity occurs. CNS toxicity (seizures) can still occur with an excessive dose or accidental intravascular injection. Hypotension and bradycardia can follow a high neuraxial block from sympathetic blockade, exactly as with any neuraxial local anaesthetic. Motor block still occurs at higher concentrations and can delay ambulation after a regional technique if the dose isn't chosen carefully for the goal at hand.

## 45. Chloroprocaine (Nesacaine) — [Category: LOCAL]
- **Classification**: Aminoester • Ultra-short-acting local anaesthetic (rapid plasma pseudocholinesterase clearance)
- **Tagline**: The fastest-clearing local anaesthetic in clinical use — metabolised in the blood itself, not the liver
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Nesacaine (chloroprocaine hydrochloride) injection label (Sintetica/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for surgical epidural anaesthesia, a 2–3% solution, with dose and volume determined by the block and level required. It was historically avoided for spinal anaesthesia in the US, largely because of neurotoxicity concerns tied to preservatives and antioxidants in older formulations rather than the chloroprocaine molecule itself — modern preservative-free formulations have since reopened some off-label intrathecal use in select practice.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a go-to for rapid emergency conversion of an existing labour epidural to surgical anaesthesia for an urgent caesarean delivery, exploiting its fast onset when speed genuinely matters. Preservative-free chloroprocaine given intrathecally for ambulatory or short-duration spinal anaesthesia is an increasingly supported off-label practice — typically 30–50 mg (2–3 mL of 2–3%) — valued for its fast offset in same-day surgery, though formal FDA labelling for the intrathecal route remains limited.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Neurotoxicity — arachnoiditis, prolonged neurologic deficit — reported historically with large-volume intrathecal use of earlier formulations was subsequently attributed largely to the antioxidant sodium bisulfite and the low pH of those older preparations, not to chloroprocaine itself; modern preservative-free formulations have a substantially better track record for off-label intrathecal use, though caution and careful practice remain warranted. Allergic and hypersensitivity reactions are more common with ester local anaesthetics generally, because of the PABA metabolite — genuinely important to remember in a patient who reports a vague 'local anaesthetic allergy,' since that history is much more often ester-related than amide-related. There are also scattered reports that it can antagonise the analgesic effect of subsequently given epidural opioids or local anaesthetics, an interaction that's recognised but not fully understood, most often noted with epidural morphine.

## 46. Mepivacaine (Polocaine / Carbocaine) — [Category: LOCAL]
- **Classification**: Aminoamide • Intermediate-acting local anaesthetic (minimal intrinsic vasodilation)
- **Tagline**: Similar to lidocaine but with less vasodilation — a plain solution that still lasts a useful while without epinephrine
- **Source Citation**: FDA-approved prescribing information — Polocaine (mepivacaine hydrochloride) injection label (Hospira/generic); UpToDate "Drug information" monograph (2025); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.; Stoelting's Pharmacology & Physiology in Anesthetic Practice, 5th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for peripheral nerve block, infiltration, or epidural use, concentration (1–2%) and volume depend on the specific block and indication; the maximum recommended dose, per standard local anaesthetic dosing guidance, is generally cited around 4–5 mg/kg — always verify against the live label and your institutional protocol for the specific block.
- **Stated Off-Label**: It's a genuinely popular choice for peripheral nerve blocks in ambulatory or outpatient surgery specifically because its intermediate duration gives adequate surgical anaesthesia while still allowing more predictable, earlier resolution of motor block than bupivacaine or ropivacaine would — useful when same-day discharge depends on the patient regaining function promptly. This is really a practice pattern within its labelled peripheral-block indications rather than a distinct off-label use in the strict sense. Intrathecal mepivacaine (isobaric 1.5% or hyperbaric 4%), though not FDA-labelled for that route in the US, is used off-label — more established in Canada and parts of Europe — for short ambulatory spinal anaesthesia, typically 45–60 mg, valued for its comparatively fast motor recovery.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Local anaesthetic systemic toxicity applies as with any amide, managed through the standard ASRA LAST protocol. Its comparatively longer neonatal elimination half-life historically raised concern about fetal or neonatal accumulation with maternal epidural use, which is part of why it's used more selectively in contemporary obstetric practice than lidocaine, bupivacaine or ropivacaine. Methaemoglobinaemia isn't a typical mepivacaine effect. Overall its safety profile is broadly similar in kind to lidocaine's, differing mainly in its longer plain duration and lower intrinsic vasoactivity.

## 47. Oxytocin (Pitocin / Syntocinon) — [Category: PREGNANCY]
- **Classification**: Synthetic nonapeptide uterotonic • Gq-coupled oxytocin receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: First-line uterotonic for labour induction and PPH prophylaxis — rapid onset with dose-dependent vasodilation
- **Source Citation**: FDA Prescribing Information — Pitocin (oxytocin injection, Par Pharmaceutical); ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 183 (Postpartum Hemorrhage); RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 52; Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed., Ch. 69 (Obstetric Anesthesia).
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for active management of the 3rd stage of labour and prevention of postpartum haemorrhage (PPH), 10 units IM following delivery of the anterior shoulder or infant, or 10–40 units infused in 1000 mL non-hydrating IV fluid (e.g. normal saline or lactated Ringer's) at 20–40 mU/min (roughly 125–250 mL/h of a 20 units/L solution), titrated to uterine tone. For treatment of established PPH, 10–40 units in 1000 mL crystalloid infused rapidly, or a controlled IV bolus of 3–5 units given slowly over 1–2 minutes (following modern obstetric anaesthesia 'Rule of Threes' protocols to prevent cardiovascular collapse; rapid IV push is contraindicated). For induction or augmentation of labour, IV micro-infusion beginning at 0.5–2 mU/min, titrated upward by 1–2 mU/min every 15–30 minutes until established labour is achieved (maximum 20 mU/min).
- **Stated Off-Label**: The 'Rule of Threes' protocol for elective caesarean delivery under neuraxial anaesthesia is widely endorsed across modern obstetric anaesthesia guidelines (SOAP/OAA): an initial slow IV bolus of 3 units over 30 seconds to 1 minute, repeated at 3-minute intervals for up to 3 total doses if uterine tone is inadequate, followed by a maintenance infusion of 3 units/h (or 10 units over 4 hours). This evidence-based regimen provides equivalent uterine tone compared with older high-dose boluses (5–10 units) while dramatically reducing hemodynamic instability, nausea, and chest tightness.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Rapid intravenous push causes severe peripheral vasodilation, acute maternal hypotension, profound reflex tachycardia, and potential coronary hypoperfusion, which can precipitate myocardial ischaemia or cardiac arrest in women with cardiac disease or hypovolemia. Because oxytocin shares structural homology with antidiuretic hormone (arginine vasopressin), prolonged continuous infusions at high doses (> 20 mU/min) with hypotonic fluids (e.g. 5% dextrose) can cause severe water intoxication, maternal hyponatremia, cerebral oedema, and seizures. Uterine hyperstimulation, tachysystole (> 5 contractions in 10 minutes), or uterine rupture can occur during labour induction, particularly in scarred uteruses or trial of labour after caesarean (TOLAC). Mild nausea, vomiting, and headache are common transient side effects.

## 48. Carbetocin (Pabal / Duratocin) — [Category: PREGNANCY]
- **Classification**: Synthetic 1-deamino-1-carba oxytocin analogue • Long-acting oxytocin receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: Long-acting synthetic oxytocin analogue — single-dose PPH prophylaxis with prolonged uterotonic action
- **Source Citation**: WHO Recommendations: Uterotonics for the prevention of postpartum haemorrhage (2018/2023 update); SOGC Clinical Practice Guideline No. 433; Health Canada / MHRA Prescribing Information (Duratocin / Pabal); UpToDate 'Management of the third stage of labor' (2025).
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA/Health Canada/MHRA-approved: for the prevention of uterine atony and postpartum haemorrhage following caesarean section under regional anaesthesia, a single dose of 100 mcg (1.0 mL) administered slowly over 1 minute by intravenous injection immediately following delivery of the infant and placenta. In jurisdictions with vaginal delivery indications (WHO/Europe/Canada), a single dose of 100 mcg IM is administered following delivery of the anterior shoulder. It is intended strictly as a single dose; repeated administration is not recommended.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Carbetocin is endorsed by the World Health Organization (WHO) and international obstetric societies as an alternative to oxytocin for PPH prevention in settings where oxytocin cold-chain storage (2–8°C) cannot be reliably guaranteed, as heat-stable carbetocin formulations maintain potency for up to 36 months at 30°C. In high-risk obstetric surgeries (e.g. multiple gestation, large fibroids, polyhydramnios), single-dose carbetocin is widely used as a primary prophylactic agent to eliminate the need for prolonged 8–24 hour post-delivery IV oxytocin infusions.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: The most common adverse effects include transient facial flushing, warmth, headache, nausea, abdominal cramping, and metallic taste. When administered too quickly as an IV bolus, transient hypotension and compensatory tachycardia can occur; it should be administered slowly over at least 60 seconds. It is strictly contraindicated prior to delivery of the infant (cannot be used for labour induction or augmentation due to risk of unremitting hypertonic uterine contractions and fetal asphyxia). It is contraindicated in patients with severe cardiovascular disease, hepatic/renal disease, or hypersensitivity to oxytocin.

## 49. Carboprost (15-Methyl PGF2α) (Hemabate) — [Category: PREGNANCY]
- **Classification**: Synthetic 15-methyl prostaglandin F2α analogue • Myometrial FP prostanoid receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: Potent second-line prostaglandin uterotonic — essential rescue for refractory PPH, strictly contraindicated in asthma
- **Source Citation**: FDA Prescribing Information — Hemabate (carboprost tromethamine injection, Pfizer); ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 183; RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 52; Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed., Ch. 69.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for the treatment of refractory postpartum haemorrhage due to uterine atony that has failed to respond to oxytocin and ergometrine, an initial dose of 250 mcg (1.0 mL) administered by deep intramuscular (IM) injection. If uterine tone remains inadequate, repeat doses of 250 mcg IM may be given at intervals of 15 to 90 minutes. The maximum cumulative total dose is 2 mg (8 doses of 250 mcg). It must NEVER be administered intravenously due to the danger of lethal pulmonary hypertension, bronchospasm, and cardiovascular collapse. For second-trimester abortion, 250 mcg deep IM repeated every 1.5 to 3.5 hours depending on uterine response.
- **Stated Off-Label**: Direct intramyometrial injection (250 mcg into the uterine fundus during open caesarean delivery) is widely practiced by obstetric surgeons when uterine atony is refractory to IV oxytocin and IM injections. While not formally labelled as a distinct administration route in the package insert, extensive obstetric literature and society guidelines recognise intramyometrial administration as an accepted emergency rescue technique under direct surgical vision.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Severe bronchospasm and acute airway constriction can be life-threatening — carboprost is strictly CONTRAINDICATED in patients with active asthma or reactive airway disease. Gastrointestinal toxicity is virtually universal due to FP receptor activation on intestinal smooth muscle: profuse secretory diarrhoea occurs in up to 60–70% of patients, accompanied by severe nausea, retching, and vomiting (prophylactic antiemetics and antidiarrheals are routinely given). Pulmonary vasoconstriction can elevate pulmonary artery pressures and cause acute right heart strain and significant ventilation-perfusion (V/Q) mismatch with desaturation. Maternal pyrexia and fever (temperature spike > 1°C in up to 12% of patients) and peripheral hypertension from systemic vasoconstriction can also occur.

## 50. Methergine (Methylergometrine / Methylergonovine) (Methergine) — [Category: PREGNANCY]
- **Classification**: Semi-synthetic ergot alkaloid derivative • Alpha-1 adrenergic and 5-HT2 receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: Ergot alkaloid producing sustained tetanic uterine tone — strictly contraindicated in hypertension and pre-eclampsia
- **Source Citation**: FDA Prescribing Information — Methergine (methylergonovine maleate injection/tablets, Novartis/Lupin); ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 183; RCOG Green-top Guideline No. 52; Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: for the prevention and treatment of postpartum and postabortal haemorrhage due to uterine atony or subinvolution: 0.2 mg (1.0 mL) administered intramuscularly (IM) after delivery of the placenta (or anterior shoulder under specific protocol). The dose may be repeated every 2 to 4 hours as needed, up to a maximum of 5 doses (1.0 mg total). Following initial parenteral control of bleeding, oral dosing of 0.2 mg (one tablet) three or four times daily (every 6–8 hours) may be continued for up to 7 days for subinvolution. Intravenous administration is strongly discouraged and must be restricted only to life-threatening emergency situations, given slowly over not less than 60 seconds with continuous arterial pressure monitoring.
- **Stated Off-Label**: In emergency surgical scenarios where peripheral muscular access or IV access is compromised during laparotomy for PPH, intramyometrial injection (0.2 mg diluted in saline) is sometimes employed as an unlabelled rescue measure, though standard deep IM remains the gold-standard recommendation due to more predictable systemic absorption and reduced risk of acute intravascular bolusing.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Acute severe hypertension, hypertensive crisis, cerebral haemorrhage, and stroke are primary life-threatening risks — Methergine is strictly CONTRAINDICATED in patients with chronic hypertension, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, eclampsia, or peripheral vascular disease. Coronary artery vasospasm, myocardial infarction, and ventricular arrhythmias have occurred, even in young women without pre-existing cardiac disease, particularly following rapid IV injection. Severe nausea, vomiting, dizziness, headache, and chest pressure are frequently reported. Concomitant use with potent CYP3A4 inhibitors (macrolide antibiotics, azole antifungals, protease inhibitors) is contraindicated due to danger of vasospastic ergotism and peripheral ischemia.

## 51. Misoprostol (Cytotec) — [Category: PREGNANCY]
- **Classification**: Synthetic methyl ester prostaglandin E1 analogue • Myometrial EP2/EP3 receptor agonist
- **Tagline**: Synthetic prostaglandin E1 analogue — temperature-stable uterotonic with versatile oral, sublingual, and rectal routes
- **Source Citation**: WHO Guidelines for the Management of Postpartum Haemorrhage (2018/2023 update); ACOG Practice Bulletin No. 183; FIGO Guidelines: Prevention and treatment of PPH with misoprostol; FDA prescribing information (Cytotec — Pfizer); Miller's Anesthesia, 10th ed.
- **Stated Dosage**: FDA-approved: originally labelled for the prevention of NSAID-induced gastric ulcers (100–200 mcg PO QID). Obstetric dosing is supported by FDA labelling (for termination) and WHO/FIGO/ACOG society guidelines: for the treatment of postpartum haemorrhage when injectable uterotonics are unavailable or have failed, 800 mcg administered sublingually (SL) or 800–1000 mcg administered rectally (PR) as a single dose. For PPH prevention in the absence of oxytocin (especially in community/low-resource settings), 400–600 mcg orally or sublingually immediately after delivery. For labour induction and cervical ripening at term: 25 mcg orally or vaginally (PV) every 3 to 6 hours, with continuous fetal heart rate and uterine monitoring.
- **Stated Off-Label**: While originally approved as a gastroprotective tablet, obstetric use of misoprostol for PPH prevention, PPH treatment, second-trimester termination, and management of early pregnancy failure (miscarriage: 800 mcg PV or SL) is universally recognised as standard of care across world obstetric practice (WHO Model List of Essential Medicines, ACOG, RCOG, FIGO). In modern obstetric anaesthesia, rectal administration of 800–1000 mcg at the conclusion of caesarean delivery in patients at high risk of delayed uterine atony is a widely utilised off-label adjuvant.
- **Key Warnings / Limits**: Shivering and intense rigors occur in up to 30–50% of patients receiving high sublingual or oral doses (e.g. 600–800 mcg), which can be distressing to the patient and interfere with postoperative monitoring. Transient hyperpyrexia and maternal fever (> 38°C in up to 30%, occasionally exceeding 40°C with high doses) can mimic chorioamnionitis or sepsis. Gastrointestinal symptoms including diarrhoea, abdominal cramping, and nausea/vomiting are common. When used for labour induction, uterine tachysystole and hyperstimulation can occur, carrying a serious risk of uterine rupture in women with a previous caesarean scar or uterine surgery (contraindicated for labour induction in trial of labour after caesarean).
